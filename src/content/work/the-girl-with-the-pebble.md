Today was Inge’s first day of primary school. For weeks, Inge had counted the days, and last night she had barely slept because she was so excited. When she heard her mum in the kitchen, Inge jumped out of her bed and carefully washed herself at the washstand in her small bedroom. Her mum had saved money and had bought her a neat, new dress for the occasion, with tiny blue flowers printed on it, and long ribbons. Inge put it on and felt beautiful. She made little dancing steps when she went into the kitchen. The fire in the stove was already burning, and on the iron plate of the stove the chicory coffee in the pot was just beginning to tremble, filling the kitchen with a warm, homely smell. Her mum glanced towards the window while pouring the coffee, then stirred the last raspberries from the garden into Inge’s breakfast porridge. Looking out of the kitchen window while eating her porridge, Inge watched the white summer clouds drifting across the wide sky, and skylarks were greeting her with their cheerful chirping. Inge couldn’t keep still when her mum braided her hair, adjusted the collar of her dress, and fastened the ribbons in a loose bow on her back. “Today is your special day Inge, you are a grown-up girl now”, she said, “so I have a special surprise for you.” She went to the kitchen cupboard, opened the drawer and produced a beautiful tortoise-shell hair clip, which she clipped into Inge’s hair. Inge gasped with delight and gave her a hug. Her mum gently stroked Inge’s head and gave her a kiss. “Remember, you are just like all the other children”, she said when they left their cottage. Inge was imagining what the children might say to her, whether she would finally make friends, oh, school must be a magical place. Holding her mum’s hand, Inge skipped along the road. They passed Grethe Jensen’s house, and Mrs Jensen was at the front to sweep the flagstones. Inge said “Good morning, Mrs Jensen” and added proudly, “I am going to start school today, look at my new dress.” Mrs Jensen paused with the broom in her hand for a moment, then went on sweeping without answering. Maybe Mrs Jensen has a headache, Inge thought. Her mum said Mrs Jensen often had headaches, since her husband had been killed a few years earlier. Inge couldn’t really remember him. A breeze from the sea picked up and chilled the air. They turned into the school lane. The clouds obscured the morning sun. And why was her mum’s hand shaking? Inge stopped skipping and suddenly didn’t feel so secure anymore. Maybe school isn’t magic after all? She touched her smooth beach pebble that she always had in her pocket. The stone felt round and safe, and would help her today. Her mum walked her to the school gates, and although Inge had hoped that she would stay with her for just a little longer, her mum rushed off to her shift at the local dairy cooperative. Inge was on her own. The schoolyard was full of children, running and laughing. But no one acknowledged Inge’s presence, no one invited her to join in, no one even said hello to her. The school bell rang, and Inge went into the classroom with everyone else. Quickly everyone sat down at the bench desks, but when Inge wanted to sit down next to a girl she knew by sight, the girl said quickly “You can’t sit here, this is for my friend Hanne”, and the girl opposite placed her hand on the seat next to her “No, you can’t sit here, this seat I keep free for Birthe.” In the end, Inge had to sit at a desk at the back, by herself. She didn’t know what to think. The uneasy feeling from the morning that school might not be a magical place after all became stronger. Inge wanted to hide, and in a sense, she was hidden as the other children seemed to ignore her, as if she wasn’t in the classroom with them. At breaktime, the children ran outside into the schoolyard again. Inge was standing close to the school building eating her apple, when suddenly a group of older boys cornered her and shoved her into the gravel. Everything stopped. During the fall, Inge’s dress got tangled in a wooden board leaning against the wall and ripped open. It had all happened so quickly, Inge didn’t have time to react. Only when she was lying on the gravel did she hear the boys shouting ‘Don't touch her, she's infected.’ Inge was shocked, why did these boys push her to the ground? She looked around. All the children were watching her. Miss Christensen, the teacher on break duty, was in the yard too. Her gaze moved over the children as if counting them. Surely Miss Christensen would help her, and reprimand the boys. But she didn’t call out. Was it because the boys had said she was infected? Inge didn’t understand, she had seen the doctor only a few weeks ago, and he had said that she was perfectly healthy. What illness did she have that everyone avoided her? Some contagious disease that the doctor hadn’t spotted? Only when Inge got up and wanted to clean her dirty new dress, she saw the big tear at the side. This was too much for her. She started crying. And worse, when she started crying, all the children laughed at her, and shouted “You dragon-spawn, your mother was a German whore.” Inge didn’t know what a whore was, and she loved dragons, but she understood that the words were meant as an insult. Inge was upset and hurt. She looked at her torn dress and felt a tightness in her stomach. She didn’t want her mum to see it like that. How could she ever tell her what happened? Her mum would certainly scold her for not being careful enough. During the lessons, Inge kept squeezing her beach pebble and tried to keep calm by touching its smooth, round surface. She mustn’t cry, she said to herself, continue crying made things worse, this Inge had learnt immediately. Confused, hurt and distraught Inge finished her first school day.

It had been entirely different from how she had imagined it to be. On the way home, Inge could no longer hold back her tears and cried silently. When Inge came home later in the day, she spotted that one of the glass panels of the kitchen window was broken. She entered the house and found her mum sitting at the kitchen table, her head in her hands, which were raw from scrubbing milk churns. She looked very tired, she had come home early from work at the dairy cooperative and was still wearing her factory clothes. Her mum looked up, quickly rubbed her eyes and came to greet her. Inge didn’t know what to do or what to say, just that she must avoid her mum seeing her torn dress. “What happened to the window, mum?” she asked, seeing glass shards everywhere on the kitchen floor. Her mum stopped in her movement, sighed, and shook her head gently. Her mum tried to say something, but hesitated. Several seconds passed in silence. Then she finally said, “I am going to clean the kitchen this afternoon, and I am going to ask Svend Thomsen to repair the broken panel, so all will be good again, don’t worry.” Her mum turned, with her head bowed and her left hand pressing against her forehead. Shaking her head, she went into the garden. Inge was confused. What had happened? Then she realised, with her mum in the garden, she might have the chance to mend her dress quickly. She knew where her mum kept her sewing kit. She quickly opened the drawer. Next to the sewing kit was an old tin box. Instinctively, instead of taking out the sewing kit, she took the tin box out and opened it. Inside the box were some letters and a creased photograph. The photograph showed a young man in a uniform, neatly pressed, with grey trousers and a grey shirt. Above the breast pocket, Inge recognised an eagle with the strange sign she remembered from German soldiers. The man was wearing knee-high black leather boots, a black belt, and a small cap on his head. He was smiling at the camera lens, his right arm around her shoulders. Her mum had beautiful long hair, not the short, irregular hair she had nowadays and she most often hid under a headscarf. She was laughing, too. Inge sat down at the table. Carefully, she placed the photograph on the table in front of her. She reached into the pocket of her dress, took the beach pebble out and held it in her hand. She looked at the photograph. Then at the beach pebble. Outside the broken window, Inge heard footsteps in the street, coming closer.
