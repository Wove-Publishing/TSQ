How can a show about traditional patriarchal roles, where finding a suitable husband is of utmost importance in a young woman's life, be so popular in the 2020s, when debates about feminism and women's rights are so prominent? As a *Bridgerton* fan and a self-identifying feminist, I almost wanted to dislike the show in the beginning. It seemed to contradict everything I believe in. And yet, here I was, tuning in for every new season. So I took that question and focused my master's thesis on it. That meant I got to watch *Bridgerton* and call it research.

What I found is that the female characters are far from passive. They don't simply accept the rigid patriarchal rules of Regency-era London. They discuss them, challenge them and negotiate them. This is what makes *Bridgerton* an interesting case when looking at modern ideas of feminism. While some contemporary television shows assume a certain level of gender equality, *Bridgerton* makes gender differences and expectations explicit. So what can a historical romance show reveal about feminism that a contemporary story might take for granted?

Romance and feminism might initially seem like an unlikely combination. The classical romance story revolves around a heroine and hero, the obstacles they encounter and, ultimately, a happy ending. *Bridgerton* follows these same conventions. Finding a suitable match and getting married are central storylines throughout each season. Yet the historical setting also restricts the women in their choices. Their reputations, sexuality and social standing determine what they can and cannot do. The tension between romance and patriarchal expectations is therefore part of the story.

To understand how *Bridgerton* addresses and constructs modern ideas of feminism, I conducted a qualitative content analysis for my master's thesis. I selected 36 scenes across all four seasons and analysed the four central female characters: Daphne, Kate, Eloise and Penelope. My coding frame included categories such as visibility, social judgement, autonomy, decision-making and communication. The most frequently coded category was *explicit reference to gender norms*. In other words, the scenes repeatedly showed women discussing, challenging, negotiating or rejecting the expectations placed upon them. Acting on personal desires and going against these expectations were also among the most prominent findings.

Looking at the four characters individually then revealed different forms of agency and femininity. Daphne, the romantic heroine of the first season, represents a more traditional and socially accepted form of femininity. She is highly visible and enjoys social approval, but her agency is largely exercised within the expectations of courtship and marriage. Kate, on the other hand, is much more autonomous and openly resistant to gender expectations. She makes her own decisions, embraces her sexuality and speaks her mind. Yet her independence comes at a cost: she receives considerably less social approval.

Eloise also challenges patriarchal thinking, mainly through intellectual curiosity and communication. She questions why her brothers have opportunities she will never have, such as attending university.

Penelope is the most complex character of them all. She moves from being a wallflower in the first season to a successful gossip writer, romantic heroine and independent businesswoman. Through her double identity as Lady Whistledown, she discovers the power of communication and information. She becomes visible and influential, but that power also comes with social judgement and negative consequences. Penelope's development shows that gaining agency and independence does not automatically mean gaining social acceptance.

I argue that this combination is what makes watching *Bridgerton* so satisfying. The show brings patriarchal structures into the foreground and gives its characters space to openly discuss and negotiate them. It puts these discussions in glamorous dresses, aristocratic balls and romantic storylines. The female characters can stand up for themselves, challenge expectations and pursue their own desires while still being feminine, romantic and interested in love. This is a noticeable contrast to some contemporary representations of female empowerment. Strong female leads are everywhere, and characters are often described as badass or girl-bosses. Shows such as *Sex and the City*, *Orange Is the New Black* and *Sex Education* portray women's independence in very different ways. Sometimes empowerment is represented through individual freedom and behaviours traditionally associated with masculinity. *Bridgerton* offers another perspective: women don't have to reject femininity, romance or the desire for love to exercise agency. Being feminine does not automatically mean being submissive.

*Bridgerton* doesn't portray a perfectly feminist world, nor does it dismantle the patriarchal structures its characters live in. Instead, it makes those structures visible as rules that can be discussed, questioned and negotiated. Perhaps that is what makes a historical romance about marriage markets so surprisingly relevant to a contemporary feminist audience. Sometimes, seeing the rules clearly is the first step towards questioning them.
